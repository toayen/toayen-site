What to buy someone who has everything

This is not a list of novelty gadgets. Those lists exist in their thousands and they don't help, because "has everything" is almost never a shopping problem.

When someone is hard to buy for, one of two things is usually true. Either they buy anything they want the moment they want it — so no object can be a surprise — or you don't actually know very much about their current life. The second is far more common than people admit, and it's the more fixable of the two.

Both point away from objects.

Why the usual approach fails

The instinct is to search harder: more obscure, more clever, more expensive. This produces the specific failure mode of the hard-to-buy-for person — a gift that is clearly thoughtful in intent and clearly wrong in fact. It sits on a shelf. Everyone is polite about it.

The problem is that "obscure" is not the same as "wanted". Someone who buys their own things has already filtered the entire market by their own taste. Competing with that filter is a losing game.

Four kinds of gift that still work

1. Consumable quality

Things that get used up can't duplicate what they own, because what they own is finite by definition. The good version of their ordinary: the coffee they'd never pay that much for, the olive oil, the whisky, the proper stationery. It works because it upgrades a routine rather than adding an object.

2. Time and experience

A day out, a course, tickets, a meal booked and paid for. Harder to get wrong because it doesn't need storing, and it produces something they don't already have — an occasion. The failure mode here is booking something you would enjoy, so be honest about whose taste it reflects.

3. The thing they'd never buy themselves

Everyone has a category they under-spend on out of habit rather than preference. Someone with an immaculate kitchen and a fifteen-year-old dressing gown. Someone who cycles constantly on terrible tyres. This gift requires you to have noticed something, which is precisely why it lands.

4. Something only you could have given

The photograph nobody else has. The recipe in their grandmother's handwriting, framed. The book they lent you fifteen years ago, returned with a note. These cost little and are, by construction, impossible to already own.

Notice that three of the four depend on knowing something specific about the person — not on finding a cleverer product.

The actual fix: pay attention earlier

The reason someone seems to have everything is usually that you're trying to reconstruct their inner life in a single afternoon in December. Nobody can do that. What works instead costs nothing and takes ten seconds at a time.

When someone mentions something in passing, write it down. That's it — that's the whole technique. People telegraph what they want constantly, and almost never at a useful moment:

  • "I keep meaning to get back into it."
  • "Ours is on its last legs."
  • "I've always wanted to try that."
  • "I'd never spend that much on myself."

That last one is the single most valuable sentence anyone can say in front of you. It is a gift request with the price objection already removed.

Six of these, collected across a year, and the person who has everything turns out to have a fairly specific list. You just weren't holding a pen when they read it out.

If you're buying this week

No collected notes and the occasion is imminent? Then in order: consumable quality, experience, or ask someone who sees them more often than you do. Do not search "unusual gifts for the person who has everything". That is how the shelf gets another object.
