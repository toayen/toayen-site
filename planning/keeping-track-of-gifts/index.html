How to keep track of what you've already bought people

The expensive failure isn't buying the same thing twice. It's arriving at December with no idea what to get anyone, because every good thought you had during the year has quietly evaporated.

Most people's gift system is three systems that don't talk to each other: a note on your phone, a screenshot of something you saw, and a memory of a conversation. Each is fine alone. Together they guarantee that the useful information is never in the same place as the moment you need it.

What follows is a system that survives December. It works on paper, in a notes app, or in something purpose-built. The tool matters much less than the three habits.

1. Separate the idea from the purchase

The single most common failure is treating "I should get Mum that book" and "I have bought Mum that book" as the same note. They are completely different facts, and conflating them is what produces both the duplicate purchase and the panic on the 23rd.

Give every gift a state, and only three:

  • Idea — you're considering it. Costs nothing, commits nothing.
  • Bought — money has left your account. This is the one that prevents duplicates.
  • Given — it's been handed over. Now it becomes history worth keeping.

The moment you buy something, move it. Not later, not at the weekend. The gap between buying and recording is exactly where duplicates are born, because it's the gap in which you forget.

While you're there, add a line saying where you put it — loft, blue crate. Small thing, occasionally saves a bad afternoon in late December.

2. Capture ideas where you have them, not where you'll need them

Good gift ideas almost never arrive when you're shopping. They arrive mid-conversation in March, when someone mentions the thing their old one keeps doing, or that they've started running again.

The habit that works: capture in one tap, wherever you are, and don't tidy it. A messy list you actually add to beats an organised one you don't. Two details make an idea usable months later:

  • Who it's for. An orphaned idea is nearly useless by November.
  • Why you thought of it. "Mentioned their kettle is dying" tells you far more later than a link to a kettle.

3. Keep the history, especially the failures

Most people record what they bought and quietly delete it once the occasion has passed. Keep it. A gift history answers questions nothing else can:

  • What did I get them last year? (The duplicate-prevention question.)
  • What actually landed, and what politely didn't?
  • Am I spending wildly differently on people I meant to treat the same?

That last one tends to be the surprise. Nobody intends to spend three times as much on one sibling as another; it just happens, one reasonable decision at a time, and it's invisible until it's written down.

What this looks like in practice

Set aside twenty minutes once. List everyone you buy for. Beside each, write what you got them last year if you can remember, and any idea you're already carrying. That's your starting position, and it's usually more than you expect.

After that, only two of the three take any effort: capture the idea when it happens, and change the state the moment you buy. The history builds itself out of those two, which is the point.
