A Christmas gift budget that survives December

Almost everyone sets a Christmas budget. Very few finish December inside it. The failure is rarely discipline — it's that the budget was built in a way that couldn't survive contact with actual shopping.

Here is where they break, and what to do differently.

Failure one: budgeting a total instead of a list

"We'll spend £600 on Christmas" is not a budget. It's a wish, because it contains no information about when you've overspent. You only discover the answer in January, from your bank.

A budget that works is per person. Write out everyone you buy for — everyone, including the ones you always forget until the last week — and put a number beside each name. Then add them up.

Two things usually happen at this point. The total is higher than the number you had in your head, and the list is longer than you expected. Both are useful to know in October and painful to discover on the 20th of December.

The list is the hard part, not the numbers. Most overspending is not paying too much per gift — it is buying for more people than you planned to.

Failure two: forgetting the gifts that aren't presents

The per-person list still misses a category that reliably costs more than people expect:

  • Teachers, neighbours, the postman, the person who cuts your hair
  • Secret Santa at work, and the second one with friends
  • Hosting — the food and drink you bring to other people's houses
  • Cards, wrapping, postage, and posting things abroad
  • Delivery charges, especially the ones paid for speed in the final fortnight

None of these feel like gift spending in the moment. All of them come out of the same money. Give them a single line — "everything else" — and a real number. If you have no idea what that number is, last year's December bank statement will tell you in about five minutes, and it is worth the five minutes.

Failure three: no way to see the damage while it's happening

This is the one that actually matters. A budget only changes behaviour if you can see where you stand before the next purchase, not after all of them.

So record what you spend as you spend it, at the moment of buying. Not the price you meant to pay — the price you actually paid, after the delivery charge and before the discount you told yourself you'd got.

The reason this works isn't accounting. It's that seeing "£40 of a £50 budget for Dad" before you add something to a basket changes the decision. Seeing it in January doesn't change anything at all.

What to do about the December bulge

Christmas spending is not spread evenly, and pretending otherwise is what makes January hurt. Two adjustments help more than any amount of restraint:

Start earlier than feels necessary. Not for the deals — for the pace. Gifts bought in October are chosen; gifts bought on the 22nd are settled for, and settling for something is almost always more expensive.

Set the number in November, not December. Once the shopping has started, every number you set is negotiating with a decision you've already half made.

A sensible starting point

If you're beginning from nothing, this shape holds up for most households:

  1. List every person. Include the awkward ones.
  2. Put a number beside each name, then add a line for "everything else".
  3. Total it. React to the total honestly — if it's too high, cut the list or the numbers now, on paper, while it's easy.
  4. Record every purchase at the moment you make it, at the price you actually paid.
  5. In January, look at what you really spent. That number is next year's starting point, and it's the only one that isn't a guess.

Step five is the one everybody skips, and it's the one that makes next year easier.
