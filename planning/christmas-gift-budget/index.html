A Christmas gift budget that survives December

Almost everyone sets a Christmas budget. Very few finish December inside it. Most advice treats this as a discipline problem — spend less, be strong. That misses what's actually happening.

You don't overspend because you're weak. You overspend because you're stuck.

Think about the last present you paid too much for. There's a good chance it went something like this: the occasion was close, you didn't have a good idea, and so you bought something more expensive instead.

That's the mechanism. Money is what you reach for when you've run out of thought. Price becomes a substitute for knowing what someone actually wants — and worse, you can feel it happening. Some of the overspend is guilt money, topping up a gift you already suspect is a bit generic.

Which means the most effective budgeting technique has nothing to do with budgets. It's having a good idea early enough that you never need to buy your way out of a bad one.

A well-chosen £25 present lands better than a hurried £60 one. Not most of the time — nearly always. The recipient can tell which one involved thinking about them.

Make the list before you make the numbers

"We'll spend £600 on Christmas" isn't a budget. It's a wish, because it holds no information about when you've overspent — you find that out in January, from your bank.

Write out everyone you buy for. Everyone, including the ones you always forget until the last week. Put a number beside each name, then add them up.

Two things usually happen. The total is higher than the figure you had in your head, and the list is longer than you expected. Both are useful in October and painful on the 20th of December.

The list is the hard part, not the numbers. Most overspending isn't paying too much per gift — it's buying for more people than you planned to.

Start early, for the ideas rather than the deals

The usual argument for shopping early is the sales. The better argument is that early shopping is chosen and late shopping is settled for — and settling for something is almost always more expensive.

Give yourself a long runway and something useful happens: you stop shopping and start noticing. The best gift ideas don't arrive in a shop. They arrive mid-conversation in March, when someone mentions the thing their old one keeps doing, or that they've started running again.

Collect six of those across a year and December stops being a research problem. You already know what to buy, and you'll almost certainly spend less, because you're buying the right thing rather than the expensive thing.

Record what you actually paid, while you're paying it

A budget only changes behaviour if you can see where you stand before the next purchase, not after all of them.

So record it at the moment of buying — and the price you actually paid, after delivery and before the discount you told yourself you'd got. Not the price you meant to pay.

The reason this works isn't accounting. Seeing what you've already spent on someone changes the next decision. Seeing it in January changes nothing at all.

Keep the non-gift Christmas costs separate

Cards, wrapping, postage, food, the drink you take to other people's houses — all real Christmas spending, none of it gifts. Budget for it if you like, but keep it out of your gift numbers.

Mixing them makes the gift figure meaningless, and the gift figure is the one you can actually act on. You can decide to buy your brother something different. You can't really decide to use less wrapping paper.

The January number

In January, add up what you truly spent on gifts. That number — not the one you hoped for — is next year's starting point, and it's the only one that isn't a guess.

It's the step everyone skips, and it's the one that makes the following year easier.
